# Report Design

## Purpose

Design a professional report before it is built. This is lightweight editorial
guidance, not a workflow, state machine, or OfficeCLI reference.

Use it for Word reports, briefs, assessments, proposals, and research deliverables.
Adapt the structure to the request; do not force empty sections into a short memo.

## Start With The Reader

Decide, briefly and internally:

- audience and decision they need to make;
- report purpose and scope;
- level of detail: executive, operational, technical, or reference;
- evidence available and any material limitations.

Write the main conclusion in one sentence before outlining the report. Let that
conclusion determine the order and emphasis of the content.

## Recommended Structure

For a substantial business or research report, use the following order when it
fits the subject:

1. Title and report metadata (author, date, scope, or version where useful).
2. Executive summary: decision, most important findings, implications, and
   recommended action in a skimmable form.
3. Purpose, scope, and approach: what the report covers, what it does not, and
   how conclusions were reached.
4. Key findings: group evidence by the questions the reader cares about.
5. Analysis or discussion: explain drivers, trade-offs, comparisons, and risks.
6. Recommendations: specific actions, priority, owner or timing when supported.
7. Conclusion or next steps: reinforce the decision and immediate action.
8. Appendices: detailed methods, source material, assumptions, definitions, or
   supporting tables that would interrupt the main narrative.

For a short report, combine related sections rather than repeating the same
point in an executive summary, findings, and conclusion.

## Organize For Scanning

- Use descriptive headings that reveal the conclusion, not generic labels alone.
- Keep one main idea per paragraph and lead with the point before the evidence.
- Use short sections and a clear heading hierarchy; do not skip hierarchy levels.
- Put the most decision-relevant information early.
- Separate facts, analysis, assumptions, and recommendations so the reader can
  distinguish them quickly.
- Use plain, direct business writing: precise verbs, concrete quantities, and
  qualified claims when evidence is uncertain.

## Choose The Right Content Form

Use the form that makes the information easiest to compare or act on:

| Information | Preferred form |
| --- | --- |
| Decision, implication, or risk | Brief callout with context and evidence |
| Two or more options | Comparison table with consistent criteria |
| Ordered actions | Numbered procedure or phased action plan |
| Metrics across categories | Compact table; chart only when the pattern matters |
| Roles, dates, or milestones | Action table or timeline |
| Supporting detail | Appendix or clearly labeled note |

Tables must have a meaningful title, clear column labels, consistent units, and
only the columns needed to support the reader's decision. Do not use tables as
a substitute for an explanation of what the data means.

## Visual Organization

- Use a restrained, consistent hierarchy for title, headings, body text, tables,
  captions, and callouts.
- Reserve visual emphasis for conclusions, decisions, warnings, and key metrics.
- Keep pages breathable: avoid dense walls of text, oversized headings, and
  isolated headings at the bottom of a page.
- Use callout sections sparingly for key takeaways, decision requests, risks, or
  assumptions; every callout needs a purpose.
- Give tables and lists enough surrounding context to be understood on their own.

## Recommendations Standard

Recommendations should be actionable rather than aspirational. Where the source
material supports it, state:

- action;
- rationale or expected benefit;
- priority or sequence;
- accountable role and indicative timing;
- dependencies, risks, or decision gates.

Do not fabricate owners, dates, metrics, or certainty that were not supplied or
supported by evidence.

## Pre-Delivery Review

Before handing off the report, check:

- Does the executive summary stand alone and match the body?
- Is the main conclusion clear within the first page?
- Does every section advance the report's purpose?
- Are claims proportionate to the available evidence and attributed where needed?
- Are comparisons, procedures, and recommendations easier to scan in a table or
  list than in prose?
- Are appendix details separated from the decision narrative?
- Is formatting consistent and are headings, tables, and callouts readable?

OfficeCLI implements the approved design. This skill does not prescribe
OfficeCLI commands, document syntax, or a mandatory execution sequence.
